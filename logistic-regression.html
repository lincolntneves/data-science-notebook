From minutes of delay to the chance of an event

We want to predict whether a task will finish late. Each task in the historical data has an outcome: 1 if it finished late and 0 if it finished on time. Binary logistic regression estimates the probability of y = 1 given the inputs. It computes a linear score and passes it through an S-shaped curve called the sigmoid. [1] [2]

z = b₀ + b₁ × x
p = 1 / (1 + e−z)
x · rescaled input
Here x = (minutes − 20) ÷ 10. A delivery 30 minutes late gives x = 1.
b₀ · reference point
The score at x = 0, which corresponds to a delivery 20 minutes late.
b₁ · change in score
Another 10 minutes changes z by b₁. The change in probability depends on where you are on the curve.
z · score
It can be any number. The sigmoid maps z = 0 to 50%, negative values below 50%, and positive values above 50%.
e−z · exponential term
e is a constant of about 2.718. The full formula squeezes the score into a number between 0 and 1.
p · estimated probability
Multiply by 100 to read it as a percentage. Whether to flag the task depends on the threshold.

Try it in your head. If b₀ = 0 and b₁ = 1, a delivery 30 minutes late gives x = 1, z = 1, and p ≈ 73%. At a 70% threshold you flag the task. At 80% it falls below the cutoff.

How the model fits the curve

Consider a task that actually finished late. Predicting a 90% chance of delay gives the observed outcome a high probability. Predicting 10% gives it a low one. Log loss penalizes the second case more. For an on-time task, the relevant probability is 1 − p. [1] [2]

Loss per task = −[y ln(p) + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)]

ln is the natural logarithm. When y = 1, the loss is −ln(p). When y = 0, it is −ln(1 − p). For an observed delay, predicting 90% costs about 0.11 and predicting 10% costs about 2.30. Minimizing average log loss is equivalent to maximizing the Bernoulli likelihood. The lab uses gradient descent: it starts with initial coefficients and adjusts them in small steps to reduce the loss. [1] [2]

What are odds, and why is z called log-odds?

Odds compare the probability of an event with the probability it does not occur: p ÷ (1 − p). At p = 75%, the odds are 3 to 1. The score z is ln of the odds. For each one-unit increase in x, the odds are multiplied by e raised to b₁. An odds ratio of 2 doubles the odds. For example, the probability can move from 50% to about 67%. [2]

What changes with more variables or perfectly separated classes?

Add a term to the score for each input. Interpret coefficients on the log-odds scale with other inputs held fixed. When a boundary perfectly separates the classes, unpenalized maximum likelihood can drive coefficients to grow without a finite solution. Regularization penalizes large coefficients and can help. [1] [2]

How to turn a probability into a decision

  1. Set the prediction time. If you flag tasks after delivery, you already know how late the delivery was. If you plan beforehand, use only information available at that earlier time.
  2. Choose which cost matters. If missing a delay costs more than investigating a false alarm, a lower threshold may make sense. Choose it on validation data and reserve the test set for final evaluation. [3]
  3. Look at two types of error. In the confusion matrix, a false positive is a flagged task that finished on time. A false negative is a late task that was missed. Precision is the fraction of flags that were correct. Recall is the fraction of actual delays that were flagged. When delays are rare, high accuracy can hide low recall. [1] [3]
  4. Check calibration. Among tasks assigned probabilities near 70%, about 70% should finish late in a large, comparable sample. A calibration plot helps check this. Ranking tasks by risk is a separate property. [4]

Review

The model predicts 40%. What happens if I lower the threshold from 50% to 35%?

The task is now flagged. Its probability is still 40%. The threshold changes the decision while the fitted curve stays the same.

If the probability is 80%, what are the odds?

0.8 ÷ 0.2 = 4. The odds of a delay are 4 to 1.
