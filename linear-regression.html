Start here

Imagine laying a straight ruler across dots on paper. Each dot is a work shift you already observed. Put hours on the horizontal axis and installed area on the vertical axis. The line summarizes the overall trend so you can estimate an area for a new shift.

Training means using past examples with known outcomes to find a pattern. A prediction applies that pattern to a new case. The lab uses simulated data so you can see what changes when you move one control.

Example, step by step

Suppose a simple line starts at 5 m² and rises by 3 m² per hour. At 4 hours it predicts 5 + 3 × 4 = 17 m². If the crew actually installed 15 m², the difference (actual minus predicted) is −2 m². That difference is called a residual.

What to keep in mind

The slope describes the pattern in this example. It does not establish that working longer is what caused the difference. Crew size or task difficulty might also matter.

The line equation, piece by piece

Imagine recording the hours worked and the square meters installed across several shifts. Each shift becomes a point. Linear regression fits a line to describe the expected average output for a shift of a given length. [1] [2]

ŷ = b₀ + b₁ × x
ŷ · prediction
Read this as “y hat.” It is the output predicted by the line, in m². Plain y is the observed output.
b₀ · intercept
Where the line crosses the vertical axis, when x is zero. If zero is outside the data range, treat the intercept as a component of the equation.
b₁ · slope
How many m² the prediction changes per additional hour. It can be positive, negative, or zero.
x · input
The length of the shift in hours. In another problem the input might be distance, temperature, or another measurement.

Try it in your head. If b₀ = 4 and b₁ = 5, then for 6 hours we get 4 + 5 × 6 = 34 m². At 7 hours, the prediction is 39 m². One extra hour raises the prediction by 5 m².

How the model chooses the line

To judge a candidate line, measure the vertical difference between an observed point and the prediction at the same x. This difference is the residual. If the crew installed 31 m² and the prediction was 34 m², the residual is −3 m². The line predicted 3 m² above the observed output. [2]

eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ

The subscript i means “this row of the table.” Squaring each residual keeps positive and negative errors from canceling and gives large misses more weight. The sum of squared errors is called SSE. Dividing by the number of examples n gives the mean squared error, called MSE. Both choose the same line for a fixed training set. [2]

SSE = ∑ eᵢ² · MSE = SSE / n

∑ means “add them all.” The 3D surface shows MSE for different slopes and line heights. Here the height is the prediction at the average shift length. The equation above uses the prediction at zero hours as its intercept. Both parameterizations describe the same line from different reference points.

How to calculate the coefficients directly

The slope is the sum of the products (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) divided by the sum of (xᵢ − x̄)². The bars denote the means of x and y. The intercept equals ȳ − b₁x̄, so the line passes through the point defined by the two means. The x values must vary for the formula to work. [2]

What if there are other variables?

You can add terms for crew, wall type, or other inputs. In multiple regression, a coefficient describes the change in the predicted mean when its input changes and the others stay fixed. A causal interpretation requires a design that supports that conclusion. [1]

How to use and evaluate it

  1. Define the prediction. One row can represent one shift. Record installed area at the end of the shift and use only inputs available when you want to make the prediction. If you plan in the morning, use scheduled hours when actual hours are still unknown.
  2. Compare against a simple baseline. Start with the historical mean as a baseline. Train on one period and test on later shifts when predicting future production. MAE tells you the average absolute miss in m². RMSE also reports m² and gives large errors more weight. [1]
  3. Inspect residuals. A curve suggests the straight line may miss part of the pattern. A funnel suggests uneven error variance. Consecutive shifts of the same crew may have dependent errors. Normality is an assumption for some classical inference procedures. You can calculate the line without it. [3] [4]
  4. Communicate uncertainty. A confidence interval for the mean addresses expected average output. A prediction interval addresses one specific shift and is generally wider under the same classical model conditions. Avoid extending the line far past the observed shift lengths. [4]

Review

If the residual was +7 m², was the line above or below the observation?

Below. The observed output exceeded the prediction by 7 m².

Why does adding an outlier change predictions for other shifts?

The model refits one line to all the examples. The new point can alter the slope and intercept, changing predictions along the line.
