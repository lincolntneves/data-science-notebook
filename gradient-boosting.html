Start here

Imagine improving a rough sketch by making one small correction at a time. Gradient boosting starts with a simple guess for every job. Each new little tree looks at what the current set of trees is missing and adds a correction.

Training means using past examples with known outcomes to find a pattern. A prediction applies that pattern to a new case. The lab uses simulated data so you can see what changes when you move one control.

Example, step by step

Say half the past jobs were late, so the starting guess is 50%. If a past job was actually late, its label is 1 and the difference 1 − 0.5 is +0.5. If it was on time, the difference is −0.5. These differences help the next tree find where to push the score up or down.

What to keep in mind

Corrections happen in sequence. Later trees respond to the predictions produced so far. Too many rounds can learn oddities of the practice data, so check results on examples set aside.

How it learns

Start with the log-odds of the training delay rate. At each round, fit a one-split tree to yᵢ − pᵢ, the negative gradient of binary log loss. The lab computes a Newton update within each leaf, scales it by 0.25, and adds it to the current score. [1]

Equation and terms

The math behind the idea

Fₘ(x) = Fₘ₋₁(x) + η hₘ(x), p(x) = 1 / (1 + exp(−Fₘ(x)))

F is the running score on the log-odds scale, hₘ is the new tree correction, and η is the learning rate. The sigmoid maps the updated score to a probability. A score of zero gives 50%. [1] [2]

Try it in the lab

Keep the diamond still and compare 1, 12, and 35 rounds. Look for a region that gains a new boundary and track how your example’s estimate moves. Compare with the forest to see why the number of trees means something different here.

How to read the chart

The sliders place the white diamond on the chart. Move one slider at a time. The horizontal axis shows open items and the vertical axis shows delivery delay. The controls express both measurements on a 0–1 scale, with 0 meaning low and 1 meaning high. The colored background tells you which label the model would assign at that spot.

Use and evaluate

Boosting is useful for tabular patterns that one simple model misses. Tune the learning rate and number of trees together on validation data. Too many rounds can fit noise. [2]

Review

How does this differ from the random forest lab?

The forest averages trees fitted independently. Boosting fits the next tree using what the current ensemble gets wrong.
