Random forest

Pool predictions from many randomized trees.

Start here

Think of asking several people for estimates. They each see a slightly different collection of past jobs and can ask different questions. A random forest does this with decision trees, then averages their estimates.

Training means using past examples with known outcomes to find a pattern. A prediction applies that pattern to a new case. The lab uses simulated data so you can see what changes when you move one control.

Example, step by step

If three trees estimate 20%, 40%, and 90% for one job, their average is (20 + 40 + 90) ÷ 3 = 50%. The lab uses the fraction of late jobs at each tree’s final stop. It combines these fractions instead of picking the estimate of one tree.

What to keep in mind

The trees can still share blind spots because they learn from the same original dataset. More trees can stabilize the average, but you still need to check it on new jobs.

How it learns

Each tree sees a sample drawn with replacement. At each split, the lab randomly tries one of the two features and limits the tree depth to four. Averaging helps when the trees make different errors. The random seed is fixed so the experiment is repeatable. [1]

Equation and terms

The math behind the idea

ŝ(x) = (1/T) ∑ pₜ(x), t = 1,…,T

T is the number of trees and pₜ(x) is the share of late tasks in the leaf reached by x in tree t. Three predictions of 20%, 40%, and 90% average to 50%. The forest classifies from the average. [1] [2]

Try it in the lab

Start with one tree and move the diamond across the boundaries. Compare with 11 and then 31 trees, using the same test case. Look for changes in the estimate and for patterns that depend less on a single split.

How to read the chart

The sliders place the white diamond on the chart. Move one slider at a time. The horizontal axis shows open items and the vertical axis shows delivery delay. The controls express both measurements on a 0–1 scale, with 0 meaning low and 1 meaning high. The colored background tells you which label the model would assign at that spot.

Lab

Use and evaluate

Try a random forest as a flexible baseline for tabular data with interactions. Validate performance and consider minimum leaf size. More trees can stabilize the average but do not guarantee better performance on new projects. [2]

Review

Why draw samples with replacement and change which features the trees can try?

It increases diversity. The average gains less when individual trees all make similar mistakes.
