A sequence of questions leads to a prediction

A decision tree asks questions like “Are there at most 8 open items?” or “Are there at most 5 people on the crew?” The first question sits at the root. Each answer leads to a branch. A leaf is the end of the path. In this lab, the fraction of late tasks in a classification tree's leaf becomes the estimate for a new task. [1] [2]

p = late tasks in leaf / all tasks in leaf

How the tree chooses a question

The tree tests candidate questions. For consecutive input values such as 7 and 8 open items, one possible cutoff lies halfway between them, at 7.5. Each split divides the examples into two groups. Gini impurity measures the mix of classes in each group. [2] [3]

Gini = 1 − [p² + (1 − p)²]
Gini · class mix
With two classes, Gini is 0 in a single-class group and reaches 0.5 at an even split.
p² · late class
p is the fraction of late tasks in the group under study, whether the root, a branch, or a leaf.
(1 − p)² · on-time class
1 − p is the on-time fraction. Both squared terms measure how concentrated the classes are.

Calculate Gini for both groups and weight the average by each group's size. The gain is the Gini before the split minus that average. The algorithm chooses the greatest available gain at that node and repeats. It is a local choice: the best next split may yield a tree that is suboptimal overall. [2] [3]

Try it in your head. Start with 4 late tasks out of 8. Gini = 0.50. A split sends 4 on-time tasks left and 4 late tasks right. Both groups have Gini 0, so the weighted average is 0 and the gain is 0.50.

What if we want to predict a number, such as hours of rework?

A regression tree can predict the mean of the observed values in the leaf. With squared error, the best split reduces the sum of squared deviations from the group means. This classification tree uses Gini because the outcome has classes. [2] [1]

What does pruning do?

A depth limit stops the tree from growing past a chosen level. Cost-complexity pruning compares fitted subtrees and penalizes those with more leaves. Use validation to choose how complex the tree should be. [2] [3]

How to use and evaluate it

  1. Use inputs available at prediction time. Record open items and crew size at the start of the shift. Define what counts as a delay before assembling the historical data.
  2. Compare trees of different sizes. Track training and validation metrics. If training improves while validation worsens, the tree may be learning quirks of the training data. This is overfitting. Adjust depth, minimum leaf size, or pruning. [2] [1]
  3. Check how many tasks support the prediction. A 100% leaf based on one task is a weak signal. If you use leaf fractions as operational probabilities, check calibration on separate data. [2] [4]
  4. Test on new scenarios. Small changes in the data can alter the splits, especially when several candidates are close. Monitor future performance and compare with a simple rule or baseline model. A learned rule captures patterns in the data. Causal conclusions about changing the crew need additional evidence. [1] [2]

Review

A leaf has 12 tasks and 9 finished late. What does it predict?

The late fraction is 9 ÷ 12 = 75%. By majority vote, the predicted class is late.

Which would you choose: a tree that gets everything right on training data or a smaller one that does better on validation data?

To predict new cases, validation on representative data offers the stronger evidence. Confirm the choice on a separate final test set.
